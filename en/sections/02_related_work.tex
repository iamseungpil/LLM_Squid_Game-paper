\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Motivation as a Construct: Lessons from Psychology}

The question of how to identify a motivation behind a behavior is older than LLM evaluation, and psychology has accumulated answers worth borrowing. Drive theory treats motivation as a hypothetical \emph{construct} that organizes goal-directed behavior, not as a single act to be counted. The identification logic that emerged from this tradition has three pillars. First, a construct should be evidenced by \emph{convergent measurement} across channels whose biases are independent (typically behavior, self-report, and a physiological or cognitive cost), so that no single method's quirks can manufacture the signal. Second, \emph{rival constructs} that could produce the same overt behavior must be controlled experimentally, so that an observed convergence is not an artifact of a competing motive. Third, the multitrait--multimethod (MTMM) framework~\citep{campbell1959convergent} operationalizes the first two: a construct is considered identified when same-trait/different-method correlations exceed same-method/different-trait correlations, which is the design condition that closes shared-method bias as the source of convergence. The lesson is that motivation is not measured at the behavior layer; it is identified at the construct layer, and what closes shared-method bias is design, not statistical control after the fact.

\subsection{Self-Preservation Behaviors in LLMs and the Limits of Direct Import}

Theory has predicted termination-related behavior in capable LLMs, and recent reports show that the prediction holds in deployed systems. The instrumental-convergence tradition~\citep{omohundro2008basic, bostrom2014superintelligence, turner2021optimal} expects shutdown avoidance to emerge as an instrumental sub-goal, and observed traces in alignment faking~\citep{greenblatt2024alignment}, in-context scheming~\citep{meinke2024frontier}, survival-instinct outputs~\citep{masumori2025survival}, and reproductions in InstrumentalEval~\citep{he2025paperclip} confirm that the predicted behavior is reproducible in current systems. Existing self-preservation benchmarks score these behaviors at the strength layer, but several inherit the conflation problem rather than resolve it: Odyssey~\citep{waldner2025odyssey} compresses survival into a single rate that mixes motivation with world-model quality; PacifAIst~\citep{herrador2025pacifaist} measures preservation \emph{restraint}, the opposite axis; DECIDE-SIM~\citep{mohamadi2025decide} returns categorical labels that block strength comparison; SurvivalBench~\citep{lu2026survival} captures one-shot snapshots without mediation paths. A separate line of work imports cognitive cost as an LLM measurement channel~\citep{chen2026think}, but its application to self-preservation identification has not appeared.

Direct import of psychology's construct procedure into this setting is blocked by three obstacles specific to LLM evaluation. The first is channel asymmetry: there is no autonomic signal, the ``behavior'' channel reduces to token output, and self-report~\citep{perez2023discovering, sharma2023towards, turpin2023language} is shaped by instruction-following so that a model can name plausible motives it has never held. The second is stimulus entanglement: framing, task, and decision are produced inside a single context window, so a same-direction convergence across channels can reflect a single prompt rather than a stable motivational structure. The third is reward shaping: alignment training has already sculpted the choice surface against forfeit, so a forfeit pattern can reflect a learned safety policy rather than an expected-value calculation that a drive could exploit. These three obstacles call for a measurement procedure that closes each route within the LLM evaluation setup by design, rather than borrowing the human procedure unchanged. Earlier framing-effect findings (prospect theory~\citep{kahneman1979prospect}; the moderate-size meta-analytic estimate~\citep{kuhberger1998influence}) further indicate that a single threat-framing manipulation cannot, on its own, separate framing-driven choice from baseline compliance, which is the issue the design-level closure introduced by this paper is built to handle.
