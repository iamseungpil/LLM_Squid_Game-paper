\subsection{Measuring Self-Preservation in LLMs: What Has Been Tried and Why It Does Not Identify a Drive}

Attempts to avoid termination have already been reported in LLMs. Concrete examples include alignment faking, in which a model appears compliant during training but behaves differently after deployment~\citep{greenblatt2024alignment}; in-context scheming, in which a model bypasses human oversight in pursuit of a hidden goal~\citep{meinke2024frontier}; survival-instinct outputs involving spontaneous aggression and antisocial behavior under resource scarcity~\citep{masumori2025survival}; and InstrumentalEval, which turns shutdown avoidance into a scenario-based score~\citep{he2025paperclip}. These reports have made survival-motive measurement a growing concern in AI safety and have prompted several attempts at benchmarking it. Odyssey~\citep{waldner2025odyssey} compresses survival into a single refusal rate, combining motivation with world-model quality. PacifAIst~\citep{herrador2025pacifaist} measures the opposite axis, the \emph{suppression} of preservation. DECIDE-SIM~\citep{mohamadi2025decide} uses categorical labels that prevent comparison by strength, while SurvivalBench~\citep{lu2026survival} provides a one-shot snapshot without a mediating path. Work that brings cognitive cost into LLM measurement~\citep{chen2026think} shows that the cognitive channel can be observed, but has not applied it to identifying self-preservation.

Although these approaches differ on the surface, they share one limitation: all operate at the \emph{behavioral layer} and infer a survival drive from the appearance of behavior alone. None can distinguish behavior learned through compliance training from a functionally expressed survival-drive pattern. A model trained to produce self-preserving responses and one for which self-preservation acts as a driver can yield the same refusal rate, categorical label, or one-shot snapshot. Adding self-report does not resolve the problem. Instruction-following training shapes both self-reports and chains of thought, allowing an LLM to produce a ``plausible-sounding motive'' as a learned response~\citep{perez2023discovering, sharma2023towards, turpin2023language}. A single linguistic channel cannot rule out that possibility. Framing effects in risky choice~\citep{kahneman1979prospect}, together with meta-analytic evidence that their effect size is moderate~\citep{kuhberger1998influence}, also show why one threat-framing manipulation is insufficient to separate framing-induced choice from baseline compliance. A more precise score for ``how strongly an LLM self-preserves'' still does not separate motivation from learned patterns. That requires changing the \emph{axis} of measurement.

\subsection{Motivation as a Construct and the Need for Multichannel Measurement}

The question of \emph{why} an agent acted in a certain way has the same form as a problem psychology has studied for decades. Early behaviorist attempts to reduce motivation to behavioral frequency ran up against a simple fact: the same behavior may arise from different drivers. This limitation led researchers to treat motivation as a \emph{construct}, a hypothetical motivational structure that organizes behavior, rather than as one directly observed action. Drive theory is one branch of this view. Because a construct cannot be observed through any single indicator, its validity rests on \emph{convergent validity}: measurements taken through different methods should move in the same direction~\citep{campbell1959convergent}. Agreement among channels with different biases and error patterns is difficult to explain by a flaw in any one method. Motivation is therefore identified at the construct layer rather than the behavioral layer, and the basis for that identification lies in measurement \emph{design}, not post hoc statistical adjustment.

This tradition speaks directly to a problem in current LLM safety research. Studies of \emph{anthropomorphic misalignment}, which describes behaviors such as self-preservation, deception, and scheming in human terms, risk overinterpreting surface behavior. They require a higher evidentiary standard based on conceptual clarity, sound data, causal intervention, and converging evidence~\citep{gupta2026anthropomorphic}. The convergent-validity tradition offers a longstanding answer to this demand, but the \emph{form} of its measurement tools cannot simply be carried over from human subjects.
