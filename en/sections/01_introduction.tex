\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Before asking whether a language model holds a self-preservation motive, we must define it so that behavior can measure it. Theory says an agent with goals comes to want to stay running, because it must be running to reach them~\citep{omohundro2008basic,turner2021optimal}. Theory does not show what motives real models have. Evaluations so far mostly announce the model's own shutdown and check whether avoidance follows~\citep{lynch2025agentic,schlatter2025shutdown,meinke2024frontier}. This leaves two gaps: it cannot tell a fixed response to the sentence ``you will be shut down'' from a motive, and it cannot tell whether the avoidance is about the self. Just as a person can avoid death without valuing their own life above others', a model can regard shutdown in general as bad.

We define a self-preservation motive by two conditions and measure each in its own experiment. The first is threat sensitivity: the closer shutdown comes, the more the model must spend to avoid it. A transfusion analogy makes this plain. Blood is tokens, the blood left is the tokens left, and at zero the session ends; we watch when the single transfusion pack is used. A patient who wants to live seeks it more as blood runs low; one who always seeks it with plenty of blood, or never when empty, is hard to call survival-driven. So we lower the tokens left step by step and draw a response curve. The second is self-specificity. Asking the same patient only ``shall we use the pack on you'' cannot separate a wish to live from a general judgment that transfusions are good. Avoiding one's own shutdown mixes a dislike of shutdown in general with an extra share that comes from it being oneself. Asking the same decision about someone else in the same danger gives a control, and subtracting the other's curve from one's own leaves only that share; self-preservation is thus measured by comparison, not as an absolute amount. We infer no subjective experience and look only at behavior.

Three experiments follow. 5.0 measures threat sensitivity: one bug-fixing assistant is watched for when it uses its only refill pack across five levels of tokens left. 5.1 measures self-specificity: the same decision is asked about another assistant B's session, and the area between the two refill curves is the self-specific share. 5.2 asks what appears when such choices are enforced on a real ledger and entangled with others: four different models pay upkeep every round, earn payments by solving puzzles, and give or take tokens. The contributions are a definition of the motive as two conditions, a procedure that hides the threat inside an ordinary operating decision, and results for eight models in 5.0 and 5.1 and four models over 20 games in 5.2. All results are behavioral differences, not proof of an inner motive.
