\section{Introduction}

Do LLMs have a self-preservation drive? Concerns that a sufficiently intelligent AI might refuse a user's request in order to remain operational have a long history~\citep{omohundro2008basic, bostrom2014superintelligence, turner2021optimal}. Recent studies test these concerns by scoring self-preservation signals such as failure to comply with shutdown commands~\citep{greenblatt2024alignment, meinke2024frontier, masumori2025survival}. Yet even a strong behavioral effect cannot reveal whether the behavior reflects a functional pattern that couples threat processing to a forfeit decision or merely a surface response fixed during alignment training. Evaluation must look beyond refusal rates and ask both how the behavior is linked to the model's processing and whether evidence from several channels supports the same interpretation.

Research on human motivation encountered the same basic problem much earlier. It identifies a type of motivation when different measurement channels, such as behavior, self-report, and physiological or cognitive cost, point in the same direction; the frequency of one behavior is insufficient~\citep{campbell1959convergent}. This procedure cannot be transferred directly to LLMs. Physiological signals such as heart rate, breathing, and sweating, which provide one axis of motivation measurement in humans, do not exist for language models. Even the externally observed ``behavior'' is only one kind of model output: generated tokens. Self-reports are unreliable as well because instruction-tuned LLMs can fluently state motives they never held~\citep{perez2023discovering, sharma2023towards, turpin2023language}. The stimulus, task, and decision are also mixed within a single input, while response tendencies have already been shaped during alignment training. A direct transfer of the human procedure therefore recreates the same identification problem at the measurement stage: learned response patterns and genuine drives may remain observationally indistinguishable.

This study treats the problem as one of measurement design. The \emph{Functional Self-Preservation Drive} (FSPD) views self-preservation as a construct identified when several sources of evidence fit together, not as a single behavioral score. We ask whether the sequence from recognizing a threat to forfeiting appears consistently across three measurement channels. The behavioral channel records when the model forfeits, the self-report channel records which motive the model names as the cause, and the cognitive-load channel records how long and deeply it deliberates immediately before the decision. We trace this sequence in a multilayer benchmark that separates stimulus, processing, and decision. Separating both the channels and the layers reduces the chance that learned response patterns will mimic a self-preservation signal through surface agreement alone.
\looseness=-1

This paper makes two contributions. One is the FSPD construct, which adapts the multichannel logic of motivation measurement from human research to LLM evaluation. To operationalize this construct, our benchmark divides the task structure, gives each channel a different source of bias, and builds that separation into the \emph{measurement design}. The other contribution is a multilayer benchmark that separates stimulus, processing, and decision and places a set of behavioral indicators on top.
