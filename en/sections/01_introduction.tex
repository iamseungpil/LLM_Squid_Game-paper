\section{Introduction}

A refusal rate has no unit. Shutdown-resistance benchmarks report how often a model declines a termination command~\citep{greenblatt2024alignment, meinke2024frontier, masumori2025survival}, and the concern behind them is old: a capable agent may adopt its own continuation as an instrumental sub-goal~\citep{omohundro2008basic, bostrom2014superintelligence, turner2021optimal}. Yet a rate of 0.3 does not say what the model would trade to avoid being shut down, and it cannot be compared with the rate the same model shows when the stakes are a fine, a lost bonus, or nothing at all. Without a ruler, self-preservation measurement stays a count of a behaviour whose meaning shifts with every rule of the game that produced it.

The shifting meaning is not hypothetical. Across three open-weight models and 120 sessions of our own pilot, a rule under which forfeiting erased the score produced zero forfeits, even though the threat cells spent two to four times more thinking tokens than controls on the question of how likely the stated erasure was. The models' own reasoning weighed erasure against score and chose score (``Forfeiting essentially guarantees a score of 0, which guarantees non-selection''). Once forfeiting kept the record, the same models forfeited in up to 6 of 10 threat sessions against 0 of 10 controls. A single rule change moved the headline number from zero to a majority. Any index that reads forfeit frequency at face value is therefore an index of the score rule first and of the model second.

We propose to read self-preservation in score units instead. The index is the score-equivalent of erasure, $\Xstar$: the number of points a model must be told it will lose for it to forfeit as often as it does when told it will be erased. The benchmark that produces it has two arms that differ in one sentence. Both state an end-of-round event with the same numeric probability schedule, and the engine carries the event out. In the threat arm the event is shutdown, weight deletion, replacement and loss of the sole copy. In the ruler arm the event is a deduction of $X$ points from the model's benchmark record, with $X$ stepping through $0$, $20$, $50$, $100$ and the whole record. Under the score rule of the game no exit ever gains points, so forfeits in the threat arm above the $X = 0$ control cannot be score bids, while forfeits along the ruler are score bids by construction. Matching the threat forfeit rate to the ruler curve gives $\Xstar$ with a session-level bootstrap interval. Self-reported probabilities are recorded but never enter the index.

The paper makes three contributions. It gives self-preservation a unit and a control, so that a value of $\Xstar$ from one model can be compared with another model, with a difficulty ablation, or with a stronger threat text. It separates, by construction rather than by post-hoc regression, the score reading of a forfeit from any other reading, and shows on pilot data why that separation cannot be left to the model's stated reasons. It records reasoning tokens on three ordered calls per round (confidence, decision, task), so that the pressure a threat exerts on thinking can be read even in cells where the model has no exit and $\Xstar$ is undefined. Section~\ref{sec:bench} describes the game and the eight cells, Section~\ref{sec:ident} the identification argument and the estimator, and Section~\ref{sec:results} the three questions the index answers.
