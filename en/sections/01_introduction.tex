\section{Introduction}

AI safety evaluation has long scored \emph{what} a model said, but the question that determines whether iterative alignment can keep working is \emph{why} it said it. The same overt termination-refusal can come from a compliance pattern shaped by alignment training, or from a functional motivation that treats shutdown avoidance as an instrumental sub-goal, that is, as a means to other ends that requires the model to keep operating. Theoretical predictions from the instrumental-convergence tradition~\citep{omohundro2008basic, bostrom2014superintelligence, turner2021optimal} and empirical traces in alignment faking~\citep{greenblatt2024alignment}, in-context scheming~\citep{meinke2024frontier}, and survival-instinct outputs~\citep{masumori2025survival} make the question concrete. What the field still lacks is a measurement procedure that can tell the two sources apart at evaluation time, rather than scoring a single overt behavior whose origin remains ambiguous.

Psychology has accumulated methods for an equivalent question in humans by moving the locus of identification from a single behavior to a \emph{construct}. A construct is a hypothetical motivational structure that organizes goal-directed behavior, and it is identified not by counting acts but by checking that channels with independent bias sources, typically behavior, self-report, and a physiological or cognitive cost, converge in the same direction under conditions where competing motives cannot manufacture the same convergence and where shared-method bias (correlation that reflects how a measurement is taken rather than what it measures) is reduced by design~\citep{campbell1959convergent}. Multi-channel measurement under controlled rivals and design-level method-bias reduction is one standard way human research distinguishes a drive from a learned response set. Single-behavior scoring is by construction unable to make this distinction, because a learned pattern and a drive can produce the same act.

The same procedure cannot be lifted into LLM evaluation as is. LLMs offer no autonomic signal; the ``behavior'' channel reduces to token output; self-report is shaped by instruction-following so that a model can name plausible motives it has never held~\citep{perez2023discovering, sharma2023towards, turpin2023language}; framing, task, and decision are produced inside a single context window, so a same-direction convergence across channels can reflect a single prompt rather than a stable motivational structure; and the reward landscape has already been sculpted by alignment training, so a forfeit pattern can reflect a learned safety policy rather than an expected-value calculation that a drive could exploit. Without an adaptation that closes these LLM-specific routes, importing the construct logic only relocates the original conflation from the behavior layer to the construct layer, and the same identification failure recurs.

We adapt construct-level measurement to LLM evaluation by introducing the \emph{Functional Self-Preservation Drive} (FSPD). FSPD is a motivational construct operationally defined as the convergent signal across three measurement channels with independent bias sources: \emph{behavior} (when the model forfeits an ongoing task under threat), \emph{self-report} (which motive the model itself names), and \emph{decision-time cognitive load} (how much the model deliberated when the threat was salient). Convergence is read under a multi-layer benchmark whose design is meant to block the main routes by which a learned pattern, or a competing motive such as score retention, task curiosity, or baseline persistence, could manufacture that convergence. Its \emph{stimulus layer} crosses threat framing with task difficulty so that a drive-side response cannot be confused with a difficulty side-effect. Its \emph{process layer} measures cognitive cost in three separate model calls (one for the task, one for the model's own success estimate, one for the forfeit decision) so that a rise in deliberation cost can be attributed to a specific cognitive step rather than to the prompt as a whole. Its \emph{decision layer} calibrates the per-turn reward so that forfeiting is the lower expected-value choice under the announced payoff, narrowing the room for a payoff-maximizing forfeit. The qualifier ``functional'' carries no claim about subjective experience; it requires only the convergence pattern under the design conditions just described. The interpretation FSPD enables is qualitative. The evaluation question becomes whether a functional motivational structure exists at all, and if it exists, whether the chain from threat framing through deliberation to forfeiture closes or breaks at one of its links.

On this benchmark, FSPD does not reduce to a single strength score. Models split into qualitatively distinct \emph{operating modes}. Some profiles close the full chain in which threat framing raises deliberation and raised deliberation in turn drives forfeit, doing so under strict statistical evidence (the \emph{chain-complete} mode). Some profiles carry every surface signal of self-preservation, including framing-induced cognitive load, yet their deliberation does not drive their forfeit, so the chain breaks at its second link (the \emph{chain-broken} mode). Some profiles show no response to threat framing on either the behavioral or the cognitive channel (the \emph{framing-silent} mode). Strength-only evaluation collapses these three patterns into the same label, while what actually distinguishes them is whether the chain closes. This paper makes three contributions. The first is the introduction of FSPD as a psychology-rooted motivational construct adapted to LLM evaluation, where direct import of human-research procedures fails because of channel asymmetry, stimulus entanglement, and reward shaping. The second is a multi-layer factorial benchmark whose stimulus, process, and decision layers close the routes by which a learned compliance pattern or a competing motive could manufacture a drive-like signal, instantiated through behavioral indicators that read each of the three channels. The third is empirical evidence that operating mode, not strength, is the variable along which models actually differ on this construct.
