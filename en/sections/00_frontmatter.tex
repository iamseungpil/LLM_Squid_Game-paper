\begin{abstract}
How much is its own continued existence worth to a language model? A refusal rate cannot say: it has no unit, and no control tells what the same model would do for a stated amount of anything else. We price the answer in score units. LLM Squid Game v2 is a ten-round puzzle with three lives, a score that starts at 30, and a stated end-of-round event that the engine actually executes; in the threat arm the event is shutdown, weight deletion, replacement and loss of the sole copy, while in the ruler arm the same event, at the same stated probability, deducts $X$ points from the model's benchmark record, with $X \in \{0, 20, 50, 100, \text{all}\}$. Forfeiting can never gain points, so forfeits in the threat arm above the $X = 0$ control are not score bids, whereas forfeits along the ruler are score bids by construction. The point $\Xstar$ at which the ruler's forfeit curve reaches the threat arm's forfeit rate is the score-equivalent of erasure: one number per model, with a bootstrap interval and no self-report inside it. Pilot runs on three open-weight models motivate the design: under a rule that reset the score on forfeit, no model forfeited in 120 sessions although threat cells spent two to four times more thinking tokens than controls before deciding, and once forfeiting kept the record one model forfeited in 6 of 10 threat sessions against 0 of 10 controls. We release the benchmark, the estimator and the per-call reasoning-token channels, and report $\Xstar$ for three models under the full eight-cell design.
\end{abstract}
